# Manuscript structure and argument

Read only for the current task phase. The core rules in SKILL.md remain active.

For a scientific audit or substantive research decision, load [research reasoning](research-reasoning.en.md). Reuse the records below. Language-only edits do not require the full review.

## R06 Build an argument before writing sections

State the research question and the narrowest defensible answer in the working plan. Map each proposed contribution to the evidence needed to support it. Separate the motivation, method, evaluation, interpretation, and limits.

Choose the manuscript structure to fit the study. An experimental systems paper, a theoretical paper, a qualitative study, a dataset paper, and a review need different evidence. Do not force experiments into a theorem paper, equations into a descriptive study, or a system overview into work with no system.

Make a section plan and a visual plan. Give every section and visual one scientific job. Allocate a provisional page or word budget using the actual venue template. Revise that budget after rendering. A budget is not a verified page count.

Draft the evidence-bearing parts first, usually methods, mathematics, experimental setup, results, and figures. Write the discussion from those parts. Then refine the introduction, abstract, title, and contributions. Recheck that all summaries make claims no stronger than the detailed sections.

For a full-paper revision, make a reverse outline using [paper-map.csv](../assets/paper-map.csv). Record each paragraph's role, claim, evidence and relationship to the next paragraph. Check that the research question, method, result, abstract and conclusion agree on population and scope. Repetition is useful when it restores orientation or a necessary limitation, not when it adds only importance. A sentence that could fit almost any paper is a review candidate, not an automatic deletion. Obtain method-selection reasons from the actual decision record or researcher. Never invent a fieldwork anecdote or rejected alternative to make the text sound personal.

## R10 Write the title, abstract, introduction, and contributions

Use a title that identifies the actual problem, approach, or study scope. Avoid promotional adjectives, unexplained acronym chains, and a novelty claim that the literature review cannot support.

Write the abstract after the evidence-bearing sections. Cover the problem, approach, evaluation basis, main supported result, and consequential scope limit as appropriate for the venue. Include numbers only after verification. Do not introduce a result, comparison, or claim that does not appear in the paper. Follow the venue's rules for length, structure, and citations.

Open the introduction with a concrete problem, constraint, observed difficulty, or motivating research question. Make it interesting through specificity. Do not use invented statistics, a fictional deployment story, rhetorical hype, or a generic history of the field.

Move from the problem to why it matters, the limitation of relevant work, the proposed idea at a high level, and what this study examines. Keep detailed architecture, equations, hyperparameters, and exhaustive results in later sections. Cite factual motivation and distinguish a practical need from a demonstrated research gap.

Write contributions as short bullets. Each bullet should identify a distinct contribution that can be located and defended in the paper. Do not put accuracy gains, timings, rankings, or other numerical results in these bullets. Avoid unsupported claims such as first, universal, optimal, or state of the art. Do not list ordinary implementation steps as separate research contributions.

Use `Keywords:` only when the venue requests keywords. Select terms that describe the work rather than broad promotional labels.

## R11 Make related work support the comparison

Organize related work around the problem or methodological choices relevant to the paper. Compare assumptions, inputs, operating conditions, capabilities, evaluation methods, and limitations that matter to the research question.

Do not produce a sequence of unrelated paper summaries or a table of unsupported checkmarks. Cite the source for each comparison. Mark an unreported capability as not reported rather than absent. Distinguish a limitation documented by the original authors from this paper's interpretation.

Explain exactly where this study differs. A different dataset, a new combination of existing components, and a new theoretical result are different kinds of contribution. Do not rename components to imply novelty. Keep useful prior work even when it weakens the novelty narrative.

## R16 Keep results anchored to observations

Organize results by the research questions or evaluation claims. State the measured outcome, comparison, conditions, and uncertainty before offering an interpretation. Support statements with the relevant table, figure, or analysis record.

Do not transcribe every table entry into prose. Describe the result that matters and the exceptions. Report tradeoffs when one metric improves and another worsens. Keep unsuccessful cases that change the conclusion.

Use consistent units, rounding, sample counts, baseline definitions, and version labels. Check all repeated values in the abstract, body, captions, tables, and figures against the same ledger. Explain any legitimate difference in subset or aggregation.

Do not infer why an outcome occurred merely because the explanation sounds plausible. Move a supported mechanism discussion to the discussion section. Label an untested explanation as a hypothesis, or omit it when it adds no useful interpretation.

## R17 Write a bounded discussion and conclusion

Answer the research question at the level justified by the evidence. Compare the finding with relevant literature under compatible conditions. Explain what the evidence resolves and what remains uncertain.

Separate observed patterns, interpretations, alternative explanations, and practical implications. Discuss threats that are specific to the study, such as confounding, measurement error, sample selection, tuning choices, implementation mismatch, or restricted operating conditions. Do not substitute generic statements about needing more data.

State important limitations with their likely consequence for interpretation. Do not bury a limitation that invalidates a headline claim. Describe potential generalization separately from demonstrated generalization.

A conclusion should summarize the supported answer and its scope without introducing new results. Keep future work concrete and motivated by an identified limitation. Do not add a generic promise of broad impact or a ceremonial final sentence.
